Case Study: Backtest Lab

Does trend-following actually beat just holding the market?

A quantitative backtesting project testing three trading strategies against six years of real market data, including a validation step most backtests skip.

Stack: Python, pandas, FastAPI, Chart.js Data: yfinance Period: 2019 to 2025
01. The Question

Why bother testing a trading strategy at all?

Trend-following strategies like moving average crossovers are some of the oldest ideas in trading: buy when the trend turns up, sell when it turns down. They're intuitive, and it's easy to assume "reacting to the trend" should beat doing nothing.

This project set out to test that assumption directly: simulate a Moving Average Crossover strategy and an RSI Mean-Reversion strategy against real historical price data, and compare both to the simplest possible baseline, Buy & Hold, to see whether either one earns its complexity.

02. Method

Building the backtesting engine

The system pulls daily price history via yfinance, generates buy/sell signals from each strategy's rules, then simulates day-by-day portfolio value, tracking cash and holdings separately and applying realistic trading costs (transaction fees and slippage) on every trade, rather than assuming free, instant execution.

Performance is judged on five standard metrics: total return, CAGR (smoothed annual growth), Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk), max drawdown (worst peak-to-trough loss), and win rate.

03. Initial Results

Buy & Hold won on every measure

Tested on SPY from 2019 to 2025, a period that included the COVID crash and an unusually strong recovery, the baseline outperformed both active strategies:

157.46%
Moving Average Return
74.56%
RSI Return
51.70%

Both active strategies are inherently laggy: they only react after a trend has already shifted, which cost them dearly during the market's fastest recovery days. RSI mean-reversion additionally struggled because SPY, a broad index, rarely swings hard enough to hit classic "oversold/overbought" thresholds. That mismatch between strategy and asset became clearer when the same RSI strategy was re-tested on the far more volatile TSLA, where win rate improved modestly but still underperformed during TSLA's sustained uptrend.

04. Parameter Sweep

Tuning the moving average windows

Rather than testing one window pair, an automated sweep tested eight short/long window combinations. The best performer by Sharpe ratio was a fast 10/100 window, notably outperforming the "textbook" 50/200 golden-cross setup, which produced both a weaker Sharpe ratio and the worst drawdown of any combination tested.

05. Validation

The finding that mattered most: overfitting

Picking "the best" combo from a single sweep has a hidden flaw: it's chosen using the very data it's then judged against. To test whether the 10/100 combo reflected a real, repeatable edge, the data was split into a training period (2019 to 2022, used to select the combo) and a completely untouched test period (2022 to 2025).

Result

Sharpe ratio dropped from 1.37 (training) to 0.84 (test), a roughly 39% decline on data the strategy had never seen. Total return roughly halved, from 70.11% to 29.08%.

This doesn't mean the strategy is worthless — a Sharpe of 0.84 out-of-sample is still reasonable. But the gap between "how good it looked" and "how good it actually was" is exactly the risk that naive parameter optimization creates, and exactly why walk-forward validation belongs in any real strategy development process, not just this project.

06. Takeaways

What this project demonstrates

  • Building a full backtesting pipeline from raw price data to performance metrics, including realistic trading costs
  • Comparing trend-following vs. mean-reversion approaches, and diagnosing why each underperformed rather than stopping at the numbers
  • Recognizing that strategy performance is asset- and period-dependent, not a universal property of the strategy itself
  • Applying train/test validation to catch overfitting — the same discipline used in machine learning model evaluation, applied here to strategy parameter selection
